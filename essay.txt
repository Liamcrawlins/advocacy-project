AI-Assisted Work Can Still Be Creative

I handed someone my phone with Pandora's Box, the mobile game I've been building. They solve the first puzzle, watch the box beautifully open, the smaller box from inside nicely forms into a flower, and they say, "Wait, that's so cool." Then I tell them it was made with AI. Their faces change. "Oh, so AI made it.”

This one word seems to cause an issue. Although the game has not changed, its perception has. It seems that this response is very common. When presented with a series of AI-created paintings, with random paintings labeled as human-created, people believed that human-created paintings were more beautiful, meaningful, and valuable (Bellaiche et al., 2023). In terms of music, many audiences and musicians view AI-assisted composition as "not just uncreative but anti-creative" (Sanneh, 2026). I can understand this sentiment, but I think it is misplaced. AI-assisted creation is creative, as long as decisions, edits, and ownership lie in the hands of humans.
Starting From Nothing? Impossible

In product design, before designing a heart rate monitor, I had to gather examples. Within an hour, I found many examples of things that had nothing to do with heart rate monitoring. For example, a macaron, a Nintendo 3DS, buttons, screens, etc. Although none of these images were created by me, when designing my concepts, I felt that the designs were entirely my own. I chose to keep the circular shape of the macaron and simple buttons from the 3DS.

All creators will be influenced by previous work. Without having played Monument Valley as a child, I would not have created Pandora’s Box. It was so enjoyable to interact with the shapes and watch them fit together. Watching the character and the plot grow little by little. Similarly to my inspiration board, Pandora’s Box was influenced by earlier work. Margaret Boden (1998), a cognitive scientist, refers to this as combinatorial creativity, creating new combinations of existing ideas. She also predicted in 1998 that computers would be able to easily create these combinations, but would struggle to decide which combinations should be kept. It is this evaluation of combinations that is creative.
What Judging Actually Looks Like

Throughout creating Pandora’s Box with Claude, I was heavily involved in evaluating the ideas. We discussed the difficulty of each level and UI layout. In some instances, Claude suggested ideas I would not have thought of; however, in others it suggested ideas I strongly disliked. Once we had completed the 3D components, Claude had used very bright colors throughout the game. I had to change this, suggesting “Would it be possible for simpler colors to make this come to life?” and specifying exact tokens. Similarly, when creating the UI, I had to be heavily involved. The number of buttons, position, and icon style all had to be considered. Claude struggled with this idea, and I had to fight very hard to get the UI to match my vision.

This is where creativity lies. Although I did not create the ideas, the decisions to change the colors of the game and move the buttons were made by me.

Research supports this. Over 4 million artworks were analyzed in a study (Zhou and Lee, 2024), and it was found that artists create around 25% more artwork and get more favorited with the introduction of AI image generation. However, the average originality of their ideas decreased. Artists with high-quality outcomes were those who generated a wide variety of ideas, but were selective in what they finally used. Thus, while AI-assisted creation does not reduce creativity in the best artists, skipping evaluation does.
The Case Against It

Contrary to this opinion, many different concerns have been raised. In The Atlantic, Spencer Kornhaber (2026) discusses how AI tools are causing ‘AI slop’, separating ‘the concept of art from producing art’. Similarly, in his essay, Uriah Kim (2026) is concerned that people will confuse ‘fluency’ with ‘understanding’ and ‘pattern’ with ‘meaning’ (p.54). Within Melbourne, thirty artists and designers were interviewed, and many had small critiques of AI within their practice. For example, many artists made deliberate efforts to ensure that elements of their work were not produced by AI, such as making imperfect hand-drawn lines or working with physical materials. This was to demonstrate that creating should not be easy.

Despite this ‘slop’, it occurs in situations where there is no critique of the work, such as when a single prompt is used and whatever output appears from AI is simply posted. Therefore, this is not a problem with the tool itself, but rather with the person using it. However, within the work of artists in this study, many imperfect processes were still very apparent. Despite issues during my interaction with the UI, I learnt more about design in this period than when everything ran smoothly. The difficulty in using AI did not disappear, it just shifted.

Holly Herndon and Mat Dryhurst, artists discussed by Kornhaber, trained an AI to sing, based on recordings of Holly Herndon’s voice and volunteer voices (Kornhaber, 2026). While this could be seen as a remixed piece, it can be seen as their own work. As they chose what information went into the AI, and what quality of output was considered acceptable, we can consider it their own work.
Calling It Mine

In an interview with 52 computer scientists and artists, it was found that some artists view AI-assisted creation as co-creative (Wingström et al., 2024). I can understand this; however, I feel it is more similar to using tools such as pencils. Just as we do not attribute creation to a pencil, we do not consider architects uncreative for using software tools rather than rulers.

Next time you see a piece of work created with an AI and someone says “oh,” try to ask “Who made decisions?” Who removed the bright colors? Who moved buttons? Who saw potential in the macaron to create a heart rate monitor? If the answer is someone, then they have created something. The AI was simply a tool to help them do so.
